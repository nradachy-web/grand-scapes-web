## Mulch depth and placement

**2 to 3 inches is enough; 1 inch to top up; keep it off perennial crowns; no volcanoes.**
MSU Extension, Bert Cregg, "Mulching landscape beds FAQs" (2013). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulching_landscape_beds_faqs [capture 2026-05-13]
- "Deeper is not always better: 2 to 3 inches of mulch is adequate to get the maximum mulch benefit in most cases. If a site already has mulch from last year, a thin, 1-inch top-dressing is more than adequate."
- "When mulching perennials in the landscape, take care not to spread mulch over the crown of the plants."
- "Never pile up mulch at the base of a woody plant, giving the "mulch volcano" effect."
- "Neatly applied mulch can improve contrast between beds and lawn, providing a cleaner-looking edge."
- "Providing a buffer between mowers and weed wackers reduces the likelihood of trunk damage to trees."
- "In a research trial in southwest Michigan, we found that 3 inches of wood chip mulch reduced midday soil temperature by 18 degrees Fahrenheit."
- "In Michigan, red pine bark and hardwood bark are ideal alternatives since there are by-products of our local timber industry." (alternatives to cypress)
- "Inorganic mulches such as rock or shredded tires do not need to be replaced, but do not add organic matter to the soil."

**New bed 3 to 4 inches, then about 1 inch a year; 6 inch bare ring at trees; caked mulch sheds water.**
MSU Extension Smart Gardening tip sheet, Finneran, Krans and Walton, "Smart Gardeners Improve Their Soil and Weed Control with Organic Mulch". https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/smart_gardeners_improve_their_soil_and_weed_control_with_organic_mulch [capture 2026-05-18]
- "In a new landscape bed, use 3-4 inches of mulch initially, then topdress each year with only about 1 inch."
- "When gardener's heap on thick layers of mulch every season, plants, especially perennials, can be "smothered." Rots may be encouraged with too much organic material at the plant's crown."
- "As the mulch dries out, it cakes together and can become a layer resistant to rainfall and irrigation. This "hydrophobic" zone in the mulch will need to be broken up manually with a garden fork or hoe before water can penetrate it."
- Photo caption: "Trees should have a 6-inch, mulch-free ring around them."
- "Mulches applied near planting beds and trees have the added benefit of decreasing mechanical damage from lawn mowers and string trimmers."
- "Mulch can be coarse, ground chips; color-enhanced; recycled wood; or shredded bark, so choosing a type of mulch is often based on personal preference and appearance."
- "Mulching may decrease the need for watering, but does not replace it."

**Volcanoes invite rot; yearly top ups 1 to 2 inches max; break up crusted mulch.**
MSU Extension, Rebecca Finneran, "Mulch overload!" (2010). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulch_overload [capture 2026-05-16]
- Volcanoes: "this dark, moist environment is an invitation for root rot and insect invasion. It also increases surface rooting into the mulch itself"
- "The mulch should be applied as a thin 1-inch layer immediately next to the trunk or stems, which gradually gets thicker as you move outward."
- "If you are going to spruce up the beds every year, do it modestly, apply 1 to 2 inches max. Where mulch applications have become crusted and hard, break up the clods and pull excess out to the edge of the bed or drip line."

MSU IPM, Joy Landis, "Nightmare results for appliers of volcano mulch" (2008). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/nightmare_results_for_appliers_of_volcano_mulch [capture 2025-11-12]
- An otherwise healthy ash with volcano mulch blew over in a storm; "Be kind to your trees, keep that mulch back off the trunk."

MSU Extension, Rebecca Finneran, "Mulch madness" (2007). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulch_madness [capture 2026-03-13]
- "When mulch is placed in a landscape bed at approximately a three-inch depth, annual and some perennial weeds may be simply smothered. Lightly re-mulching on an annual basis prevents newly deposited seeds from germinating."

## Landscape fabric under mulch (the "black mesh" in Bob S.'s review)

MSU Extension, "Select the right garden mulch" (2025-11-12). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/select_the_right_vegetable_garden_mulch [capture 2026-02-12]
- "These products are sometimes used as a base layer under other mulches in gardens. For long-term use, they perform this function poorly."
- "When placed in perennial beds, they can become a nightmare. Over time, weeds and plant roots will penetrate and grow through the layers, making it impossible to remove without a serious amount of work and cutting. They also limit water infiltration, leading to soil compaction when used over long periods of time."
- "Shredded bark ... is one of the most common types of bark mulches in ornamental beds. Dyed and undyed options are available."

MSU Extension, "Planning your weed control programs" (2006). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/planning_your_weed_control_programs [capture 2026-02-07]
- "Landscapes. In general, use of synthetic fabrics plus mulch will control most weeds in a visually pleasing manner."
- Fabrics: "Most of these are fairly expensive, but this cost may be warranted in landscape beds"

Use on the site: fabric plus mulch is a sound way to hold weeds down in shrub beds; MSU warns that in perennial beds weeds and roots grow into it, it is hard to pull out later, and over years it can slow water. No herbicide wording on the site (checker and FACTS).

## Pruning shrubs

MSU Extension, Bob Bricault, "Pruning spring flowering shrubs" (2013). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/pruning_spring_flowering_shrubs [capture 2026-01-24]
- "Lilacs, as well as forsythia, spirea, weigela, viburnums and many other spring-flowering shrubs, form their flower buds the previous summer and pruning them in early spring would remove most of these flower buds."
- "Spring flowering shrubs are best pruned right after they are done blooming."
- "It is always good to remove dead and dying branches prior to new growth coming out in spring."
- "Plants may not need to be pruned each year." Reasons to prune listed: too large for the site, damaged stems, thin at the base, overgrown with crowded stems.
- Lilac "blooms best when it has six or more hours of full sunlight."

MSU Extension, updated from Mary Wilson, "Spring shape up: Tidying up your plants" (2011). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/spring_shape_up_tidying_up_your_plants [capture 2025-12-12]
- "For many shrubs, the ideal time to prune is late winter or early spring before new growth begins. However, hold off pruning spring-flowering shrubs, such as forsythia (Forsythia) and lilac (Syringa), until right after they flower."
- "Summer blooming shrubs are best pruned in late winter or early spring." Examples: rose-of-Sharon and Annabelle hydrangea (bloom on current season's growth).
- Foliage shrubs such as alpine currant, dwarf burning bush and privet "are also best pruned in late winter or early spring."
- "In general, deciduous shrubs should be thinned out rather than sheared back." Thinning "is best done with hand pruning shears, not hedge shears."
- Gradual renewal: "Prune plants by cutting off the oldest branches at the ground, leaving only the young stems. If there are few younger stems, remove the older wood over a three-year period"
- Total rejuvenation (all branches at or a few inches above ground) "is usually reserved for plants that have become overgrown, severely weakened or otherwise unhealthy." "Flowering shrubs may not bloom for one or several years". Shrubs that benefit: "shrub dogwoods (Cornus), forsythia (Forsythia), honeysuckle (Lonicera), spirea (Spiraea), some viburnums (Viburnum), weigela (Weigela)". "Be aware that all plants do not respond to this type of pruning".

MSU Extension, Russell Kidd, "Tips on tree and shrub pruning" (2012). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/tips_on_tree_and_shrub_pruning [capture 2026-02-09]
- "Probably the best time from the standpoint of the health of a plant is to prune in late winter or early spring just before new growth resumes for the year."
- "the least desirable time to prune a woody landscape plant is in the early summer immediately after new growth as developed."
- Hedges such as privet or yew: "It's actually better to keep the top trimmed back narrower than the bottom branches. This allows more sunlight to penetrate"
- "it's advisable to let a professional tree company prune tall trees or large diameter tree branches." Lines near electrical wires may need the utility. (Supports: big trees are not ours.)

MSU Extension, Rebecca Finneran, "Pruning evergreen shrubs" (2011). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/pruning_evergreen_shrubs [capture 2026-02-12]
- "size control is the primary reason for pruning foundation plants."
- Yew: "By hand trimming back the leggy shoots that appear each June, the plant can have a natural appearance and still stay in bounds." Deeper cuts into the "shoulders" are acceptable on an overgrown yew; the article says lower buds "will respond and grow when exposed to sunlight" and also that they "are slow to respond in most cases" (use: yews can take a hard cut, but regrow slowly from old wood).
- "keep the top of the plant more narrow than the bottom. This will encourage thickness at the base"
- Junipers: "they do not sprout from the interior branches when cut hard" ... "avoid hedging shears all together." "When a juniper has been cut repeatedly with hedge shears, it is likely to become deformed and die back beyond recognition."
- "one should always question, "Was the shrub planted in the right location to begin with?"" and "I like to keep the chain saw (for removal) in the running as an option." (Supports: sometimes removal beats pruning.)

Not used: late summer or fall shearing timing for arborvitae. No MSU page found that settles it, so the pages do not give a date for it.

## Box tree moth (boxwood) in Genesee County

MDARD press release, "Box tree moth confirmed in Genesee County" (2026-09-22). https://www.michigan.gov/mdard/about/media/pressreleases/2026/09/22/box-tree-moth-confirmed-in-genesee-county
- MDARD with USDA "has confirmed the detection of box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis) in Genesee County". "it can lead to significant defoliation and death of ornamental boxwood."
- Found "through USDA trapping conducted near the perimeter of Michigan's existing box tree moth quarantine area."
- Quarantine "now encompasses 13 counties in Southeastern Michigan: Clinton, Eaton, Ingham, Jackson, Lapeer, Lenawee, Livingston, Macomb, Monroe, Oakland, St. Clair, Washtenaw, and Wayne. Given the new detection, MDARD will update the quarantine to include Genesee as the 14th impacted county."
- Buxus yard waste may move inside the regulated area only if the destination has no Buxus within 100 yards and the waste is enclosed in transit (March through October).
- Outside the quarantine area, report suspected infestations with photos at www.Michigan.gov/ReportBTM, and "notify MDARD before spraying or disposing of the suspected infestation."

MDARD Plant Pest Quarantines page (read 2026-11-21): https://www.michigan.gov/mdard/plant-pest/plant-health/plant-pest-quarantines . The posted Box Tree Moth Interior Quarantine PDF was last modified 2026-02-12, before the Genesee find. So as of 2026-11-21 the update adding Genesee had not been posted. Site wording: "the state confirmed box tree moth in Genesee County in September 2026 and said it will add the county to the quarantine." Oakland County (Holly, Ortonville, Clarkston) is already inside it.

Michigan Invasive Species, "Box Tree Moth" ID page. https://www.michigan.gov/invasives/id-report/insects/box-tree-moth
- What to look for: "green and yellow caterpillars with white, yellow, and black stripes and black spots", "Loose webbing in the area of caterpillar activity", feeding on leaf edges or skeletonized leaves.
- Under the quarantine, Buxus plant material "including all living and dead material, cannot be moved outside of the quarantined area."

MSU Extension, Gimondo, Jubenville and Lowenstein, "What the box tree moth quarantine means for Michigan homeowners" (2023-12-13). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/what-the-box-tree-moth-quarantine-means-for-michigan-homeowners [capture 2026-01-16]
- "Moving any boxwood material from within the quarantine zone to the outside is strictly prohibited, including potted plants and trimmings."
- "Box tree moth caterpillars only feed and reproduce on boxwood (Buxus spp.)."
- Look for "sections of branches that are tightly clustered together where caterpillars may have formed a nest with webbing" and "Small, lime green frass".

MSU Extension, Kerr and Splawski, "Beyond boxwood: Alternative landscape shrubs for Michigan gardeners" (2026-03-20). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/beyond-boxwood-alternative-landscape-shrubs-for-michigan-gardeners [capture 2026-04-19]
- Alternatives named: Japanese holly, inkberry holly, junipers, yews, Green Giant arborvitae, sweetbox, and others.
- "It's not recommended to plant Privet, Japanese barberry or Burning bush for this reason" (risk of becoming invasive).
- Yews "can withstand harsh pruning, but it is a better practice to prune more frequently to prevent them from becoming over-grown before cutting back."
- Green Giant arborvitae: grows fast, "may need to be removed once they become too large."

## Invasive shrubs

MSU Extension, Filice, Jubenville and Splawski, "Michigan expands invasive plant list: What retailers and homeowners need to know" (2026-06-23). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/michigan-expands-invasive-plant-list-what-retailers-and-homeowners-need-to-know [capture 2026-07-17]
- Effective January 1, 2028: common buckthorn, glossy buckthorn, Callery pear and Japanese barberry, cultivars included, become restricted.
- "If any of these species are already present on a property or in nearby waterways, the homeowner is not in violation of the law and is not required to take immediate action."
- "landscapers should be vigilant and cautious when managing and removing these plants for clients. They should use proper disposal techniques such as those outlined in the Michigan Citizen's Guide to Invasive Plant Disposal."

MSU Extension, Georgia Peterson, "Green isn't always good" (2016). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/green_isnt_always_good [capture 2025-11-18]
- Bush honeysuckles (Amur, Tatarian, Morrow's, Bell's) "leaf out extremely early in the spring and remain green well into the fall."
- "They are shallow-rooted, and young growth can easily be pulled in spring's damp soil. Any remaining roots in the ground, however, will re-sprout easily. The same goes if the stems are clipped, sprouting is vigorous."

MSU Extension, Bill Cook, "Eliminating buckthorn will improve the habitat on any property" (2017). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/eliminating_buckthorn_will_improve_the_habitat_on_any_property [capture 2026-04-21]
- "Come spring, the buckthorn stumps sprout back vigorously."
- Fruit clusters turn red to black; "Some folks mistake them for cherries or wild plums."

## MISS DIG 811 (calling before digging)

MISS DIG 811, "Digging FAQs". https://www.missdig811.org/get-started/i-want-to-dig/faq.html
- "Yes, you have to contact MISS DIG 811 at least 3 business days before your project. This applies to any project regardless of size or depth. This can be done by either calling 811 or going to missdig811.org." "Contacting MISS DIG 811 is free and legally required before your project."
- "If the homeowner is doing the work, then it is their responsibility to contact MISS DIG 811. If the contractor is doing the work, then it is the contractor's responsibility to contact MISS DIG 811."
- Private lines are not marked: "a utility line to a grill, shed, underground sprinkler systems, invisible dog fences, detached garage, pool, etc. They would need to be marked by Private Locators for a fee."
- "Digging needs to start within 14 days and be completed within 21 days of the Work Legal Start Date."
- Hand digging within "4 ft of either side of the marks (known as the caution zone)."

MISS DIG 811, "Introduction" (homeowners). https://www.missdig811.org/homeowners/introduction.html
- "A business day is defined as 7 a.m. to 5 p.m. Monday through Friday, not including holidays"
- Lines not marked include "sprinkler lines, electric to a garage, pool lines, electric fences, water well, and septic."

MISS DIG 811, "Digging Safely" (homeowners). https://www.missdig811.org/digging-safely-homeowners.html
- "If a homeowner has hired a contractor/excavator for excavation on their property it is the responsibility of the contractor/excavator to contact MISS DIG 811 for a locate request per PA 174." The Michigan Public Service Commission enforces PA 174.

MSU Extension, Elaine Bush, "Call 811 before starting any digging projects" (2013). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/call_811_before_starting_any_digging_projects_to_prevent_injury_service_dis [capture 2025-11-20]
- Lists tree planting as a project that needs the call. Flag colors: "red: electric; orange: TV, telephone, cable, fire and police; yellow: gas and oil; green: storm drains; blue: water systems; brown: sewer systems; and pink: surveying." (Older article; cites the pre-2013 law, so the site uses the MISS DIG pages for the rule itself.)
