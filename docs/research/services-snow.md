## Michigan law on snow in the road

**MCL 257.677a, verified wording.**
Michigan Legislature, MCL 257.677a. https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/MCL-257-677A.pdf (rendered October 27, 2026, "Complete Through PA 114 of 2026"); also https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-257-677a
- Title: "Obstruction of safety vision by removal or deposit of snow, ice, or slush prohibited."
- (1)(a) "Person" does not include the state or a political subdivision of the state or an employee of the state or a political subdivision of the state operating within the scope of his duties.
- (1)(b) "Safety vision" means an unobstructed line of sight enabling a driver to travel upon, enter, or exit a roadway in a safe manner.
- (2) "A person shall not remove, or cause to be removed, snow, ice, or slush onto or across a roadway or the shoulder of the roadway in a manner which obstructs the safety vision of the driver of a motor vehicle other than off-road vehicles."
- (3) Same for "deposit, or cause to be deposited", onto or across a roadway or shoulder in a manner which obstructs safety vision.
- (4) "A person shall not deposit, or cause to be deposited, snow, ice or slush on any roadway or highway."
- (5) "A person who violates this section is responsible for a civil infraction and shall be ordered to pay a civil fine of not more than $100.00."
- History: added 1978, Act 82; amended 2020, Act 382, effective Oct. 1, 2021.
- Use on the site: the rule in words, "a civil infraction". The fine amount is left off (no dollar figures on the site).

## Rock salt and other ice melts

**Melting temperatures (state agency).**
Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, Minnesota Stormwater Manual, "How salt works and overview of deicing chemicals". https://stormwater.pca.state.mn.us/How_salt_works_and_overview_of_deicing_chemicals
- Lowest practical melting pavement temperature: sodium chloride 15°F, magnesium chloride -10°F, calcium chloride -20°F.
- "Rock salt can melt to -6 oF pavement temperatures but the colder the pavement the slower it works." Best practice: "avoid using dry rock salt at pavement temperatures below 15 oF because it is too slow."
- Concrete column: sodium chloride "Low/moderate; Will exacerbate scaling; low risk of paste attack". Magnesium chloride and calcium chloride also "Will exacerbate scaling"; magnesium chloride carries a "risk of paste deterioration from magnesium".

University of Minnesota LTAP, SmartWinterSalt homeowners guide. https://mnltap.umn.edu/guides/smartwintersalt/homeowners/pavement/deicing-use
- Lowest practical temperatures: NaCl 15°F, MgCl2 -10°F, CaCl2 -20°F, KCl 25°F, CMA 20°F.
- Melt table (from MPCA), 1 lb of dry salt: 30°F 46.3 lb of ice in 5 min; 25°F 14.4 lb, 10 min; 20°F 8.6 lb, 20 min; 15°F 6.3 lb, 1 hour; 10°F 4.9 lb and 5°F 4.1 lb, "Dry salt is ineffective and may blow away before it melts anything".
- "Use sand or grit to provide traction."
- https://mnltap.umn.edu/node/24351: "A 12-oz coffee mug holds about one pound of salt—enough for 250 square feet." and "applying more salt than needed does not melt ice faster."

**MSU Extension deicer table.**
Terry Gibb, "Winter ice and deicers" (2015). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/winter_ice_and_deicers [capture 2025-12-14]
- "Michigan State University Extension recommends that you clear these surfaces of as much snow as possible by shoveling before applying any product. This will allow whatever chemical you choose to work most efficiently while minimizing the amount needed."
- Sodium chloride: "Inexpensive"; "Damages concrete; Can kill vegetation; Harmful to pets; Not effective below 15 degrees Fahrenheit".
- Calcium chloride: "Gives off heat as it dissolves which melts quicker than salt"; "Works well at very low temperatures (-25 degrees F)"; "Melts ice, snow on contact"; "Not as damaging to vegetation as salt"; "Costs more; May damage concrete; Leaves slimy residue; Corrosive on metal".
- Potassium chloride: "Melts ice to 12 degrees F"; "Over-application can be harmful to vegetation".
- CMA: "Little effect on vegetation or concrete; Effectiveness decreases below 20 degrees F".
- Sand and similar: "Effective for gritty, anti-slip qualities"; "Depending on product used will have spring clean up on driveways and sidewalks".
- "All deicers have some potential to damage vegetation and concrete and corrode metal."
- "Flush vegetation with water if spills or over use has occurred or damage appears on plants." "Do not use deicers on new concrete that has not fully cured." "Sweep up any salt or chemical residue as soon as the snow melts in the spring to reduce pollution into local waterways."
- Not used: the table's "Manganese Chloride" row (it appears to mean magnesium chloride, and its "safer on concrete" note conflicts with MPCA, FHWA and PACA).

**How deicers work.**
University of Nebraska Extension, Fitzgerald and Janssen, G1121 "Winter Deicing Agents for the Homeowner". https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1121/na/html/view
- "Do not use deicers to completely melt snow or ice, but to make their removal easier. Deicers melt down through the ice or snow to the hard surface, then spread out underneath."
- Scaling: "caused by cycles of freezing and thawing caused by the deicing agent. Water can be absorbed into the very fine capillary space in the concrete. When it freezes and expands, small flakes of mortar and concrete come loose from the surface."
- "make sure all concrete-paved surfaces are composed of air-entrained concrete and have proper cement content. Air-entrained concrete has minute air bubbles to increase resilience and resistance to wear." "new concrete should be air dried for at least 30 days prior to the first freeze. Sealers may be applied to prevent the concrete from absorbing water and salts."
- "Products such as ammonium nitrate or ammonium sulfate tend to damage concrete more than sodium chloride or calcium chloride."

## Deicers and concrete

Portland Cement Association: its own pages could not be retrieved (cement.org pages returned an index or 404). The sources below cite PCA research.

Federal Highway Administration, Tech Brief FHWA-HIF-17-008, "Chemical Deicers and Concrete Pavement: Impacts and Mitigation" (March 2018). https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/pavement/pubs/hif17008.pdf
- "It has long been recognized that chemical deicer applications amplify the physical mechanisms responsible for freeze-thaw deterioration of concrete", including "the increased level of saturation that occurs when salt solutions are present".
- Entrained air voids relieve "the stress generated as the pore solution freezes".
- "Until recently, the most common distress associated with deicer applications on concrete flatwork, such as slabs, has been surface scaling".
- Newer distress at pavement joints is tied to "aqueous solutions of calcium chloride (CaCl2) and magnesium chloride (MgCl2)".

Pennsylvania Aggregates and Concrete Association (trade association), "Protecting Concrete From Damage Due to Deicers". https://www.pacaweb.org/uploads/documents/ProtectingFromDeIcer.pdf (references include PCA Design and Control of Concrete Mixtures and PCA Bulletin 83)
- "avoid the use of deicing agents during the critical first year after placement and use the proper deicers in moderation in the years that follow."
- "Deicers should not be used on concrete less than one year old."
- "Sodium chloride or calcium chloride based deicers are generally considered acceptable for use on good quality dense concrete."
- "Never use deicers with ammonium sulfate or ammonium nitrate as these chemicals are too aggressive and will easily destroy a concrete surface."
- "Many deicers have the label "safe" in large letters on the front of the bag, however the fine print on the back of the bag lists many conditions on its use."
Same group, "Scaling" sheet: https://www.pacaweb.org/uploads/documents/PIS_Scaling.pdf ("Under no circumstances should a deicer be used on concrete that has not fully cured.")

## Salt and plants

MSU Extension, Bob Bricault, "Salt damage on landscape plants" (2011). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/salt_damage_on_landscape_plants [capture 2025-01-22]
- "Salt damage occurs on plants where spray mist from passing cars coat the leaves and buds and where large concentrations build up in landscape beds adjacent to sidewalks and driveways."
- "Sodium ions reduces root uptake of necessary plant nutrients, such as potassium, calcium and magnesium. Chloride ions are moved into the plant and accumulate in leaf tissue where it interferes with photosynthesis."
- "Sensitive plants like white pines, roses, rhododendrons and yews can become weak, stunted, have dried out foliage and in cases where salt use is very high, plants may die."
- Alternatives near sensitive plants: "coarse sand providing traction on ice". "Spring rains will help to flush soil of salt, but if conditions are on the dry side it may become necessary to protect plants by watering sites to reduce salt levels. Barriers can be placed around susceptible plants with stakes and burlap cloth".

## Salt and pets

ASPCA, "Is It Safe to Use De-Icers Around Your Dog?" (January 22, 2026). https://www.aspca.org/news/it-safe-use-de-icers-around-your-dog
- Rock salt "can be harsh on dogs' paws, cause irritation, and if its ingested, can cause dangerously high sodium levels."
- Pet friendly products "typically contain urea or magnesium chloride." "There are no specific requirements for labeling a de-icer product as 'pet safe.'"
- "wipe or rinse your dog's paws after a walk, consider having your pet wear paw booties". "Sand can be spread on walkways and driveways to provide traction." Poison control "(888) 426-4435".
ASPCA, "Keeping Pets Safe during Winter: The Dangers of Ice Melts" (December 4, 2018). https://www.aspca.org/news/keeping-pets-safe-during-winter-dangers-ice-melts
- "Wipe your pet's paws as soon as they get inside with a damp cloth or baby wipes." "Keep all ice melt packaging out of paws' reach." Some pets "will eat it directly out of the packaging if given the chance." "Don't let dogs eat any of the salt or any of the snow (especially the slushy snow) outside that may have been treated with an ice melt."

## Mowing, stripes and the last mow

**Why stripes show.**
University of Minnesota Turfgrass Science, Florence Sessoms, "Light reflection and mowing patterns" (May 1, 2023). https://turf.umn.edu/light-reflection-and-mowing-patterns
- "A common misconception is that the stripe pattern observed after mowing is a result of re-arranging the side of the leaves during the mow." "the mowing pattern observed is the result of light reflection, the size of surface area on which the light can be reflected and where the viewer stands."
- Behind the mower, grass "pushed backward (your position) increasing its surface area where sunlight can be reflected creating a lighter green"; facing the mower, grass "pushed toward you, decreasing its surface area ... leading to a darker green."
- Test with perennial ryegrass and Kentucky bluegrass leaves: "leaning backward resulted in a lighter color".
- "The stripe pattern can be re-enforced with higher heights of cut, (more surface), species with leaves that can bend easier (cool season > warm season grasses) and the use of a roller after the mow."
- No named patterns on the page. The patterns on the site (straight, curved, crosscut, checkerboard) are the ones in Rob's photos; diagonal and diamond are described only by how they are mowed.

**Mowing height, one third, direction, blades, leaves.**
MSU, G.T. Lyman and P.E. Rieke, "Turf Tips For the Homeowner: Mowing Lawn Turf" (2002). https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/turf-tips-for-the-homeowner-mowing-lawn-turf [capture 2026-03-14]
- "mowing too short will reduce the vigor of the plants by reducing their ability to manufacture food." "Lowering the mowing height reduces the root system."
- "Current standards suggest between 2 and 3.75 inches."
- "A general rule of thumb is not to remove more than one-third of the total leaf surface when mowing your lawn."
- "Dull, poorly adjusted equipment tears rather than cuts the grass, leaving a ready site for disease invasion and giving the lawn a frayed, brownish look." "mow when the turf is dry; this will also eliminate clumping. Varying the mowing direction from time to time can reduce wear patterns."
- Leaves: "up to 6 inches of various types of tree leaves were mowed into lawns. No adverse effects were detected on the lawns." "A couple of passes with the mower will break the leaves down into small pieces." Residue "will sift into the turf within a few weeks and will be unnoticeable in the spring." "You can even rake leaves that accumulate in planting beds, fence lines or other areas out into the lawn and mow them." "raise your mowing height ... and mow when the leaves are dry."
- "Current Michigan law restricts many residents from dumping yard wastes such as grass clippings and leaves into landfills".

**The last mow.**
MSU Extension, Kevin Frank, "Fall turf tips" (September 18, 2009). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/fall_turf_tips [capture 2025-11-17]
- "When you get past the leaf mulching period in the fall and the turf has essentially stopped its top-growth, it is OK to lower the mowing height to clean-up the turf and prevent the turf from being too tall going into winter. I would recommend that you lower the mowing height no more than one notch on your mower (typically a half inch). This will help you clean-up any left over leaf mulch or debris and give the turf a little trim before winter sets in."
- Leaves: sharp blade; "raise the mower as high as it will go"; "Try to mow the leaves when they are moist from the morning dew, but don't mow them when they're really wet."; "don't let the leaves pile up too high before you mow, too high would probably be greater than three to four inches of leaf depth on the turf. Mulching leaves helps the turf by returning nutrients and organic matter".
MSU Extension, Kevin Frank, "Autumn turf tips" (September 19, 2008). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/autumn_turf_tips [capture 2025-11-21]: same final mowing advice, "no more than one notch on your mower, typically one half inch."

## Leaves: mulch or remove

MSU Extension, Rebecca Finneran, "It's not too late to mulch fallen leaves into lawns" (November 17, 2014). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/its_not_too_late_to_mulch_fallen_leaves_into_lawns [capture 2026-01-15]
- "The ban on yard waste during the 90s prompted Michigan State University Extension turf specialists to investigate how lawns would react to having a whole season's worth of ground-up leaves left on the lawn". "They found lawns do better with a layer of mulched leaves."
- "Mulching mowers can handle up to 6 inches of leaves at a time in one pass, but a traditional mower might take several trips." Possibly twice a week. Residue "only lasts for a few days. The tiny pieces will eventually sift down through the turf".

MSU Smart Gardening tip sheet, Rebecca Finneran, "Mulch Leaves into Turf for a Smart Lawn" (May 10, 2016). https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/mulch_leaves_into_turf_for_a_smart_lawn [capture 2025-12-15]
- "when leaves pile up with wet, heavy snow, it can mean disaster for the turf plant below."
- "Up to 6 inches of leaves can be "mulched" at a time, depending on the type of mower you have. Push mowers will handle smaller amounts".
- Benefits found: spring green up with fewer inputs, and "nearly a 100 percent decrease in dandelions and crabgrass can be attained after adopting this practice of mulching leaves for just three years."
- Bagging mower: alternate mulching one week and collecting ground leaves for beds.

MSU Extension, Terry Gibb, "To bag or not to bag?" (October 31, 2018). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/to-bag-or-not-to-bag [capture 2025-11-14]
- "Start mowing and shredding the leaves as soon as there is a thin layer on the lawn." "Set the mower at its highest setting and mow as usual. Then mow in the opposite direction making a criss-cross or 90-degree pattern."
- "The small leaf pieces cover any bare or thin spots in the lawn that are good places for weed seeds to germinate."
- "If a total leaf fall occurs and there are too many leaves to mulch, another option for the leaves is using your mower to bag the leaves for use around flower beds, trees, shrubs and vegetable gardens."

MSU Extension, Rebecca Krans, "Don't sweep your leaves to the curb!" (November 7, 2016). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/dont_sweep_your_leaves_to_the_curb_mulch_them_back_into_your_lawn_or_garden [capture 2026-01-17]
- "Be sure you go over the leaves a number of times, making sure they are broken down into small pieces. This way the leaves will not mat together".
- If too many leaves to mow, "collect the mulched leaves every other mowing" for beds.

## Mulch depth

MSU Extension, Bert Cregg, "Mulching landscape beds FAQs" (May 2, 2013). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulching_landscape_beds_faqs [capture 2026-05-13]
- "Deeper is not always better: 2 to 3 inches of mulch is adequate to get the maximum mulch benefit in most cases. If a site already has mulch from last year, a thin, 1-inch top-dressing is more than adequate." Do not spread mulch over perennial crowns; no "mulch volcano".
- Benefits: conserves moisture, "3 inches of wood chip mulch reduced midday soil temperature by 18 degrees Fahrenheit" (southwest Michigan trial), adds organic matter, weeds, appearance, mower and trimmer buffer.

MSU Extension, Bert Cregg, "Mulching tips for the landscaper" (May 26, 2006). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulching_tips_for_the_landscaper [capture 2025-11-24]
- Trial of ground pine bark, hardwood bark, cypress and "ground pallets (the ubiquitous red mulch)": "Most of the mulches worked equally well." Cypress plots grew a little slower.
- "Two to three inches of mulch is adequate – more is not better." "keep a 6-inch mulch-free ring around trees." Uncomposted clippings or leaves "will form a thick, smelly, unsightly mat that inhibits moisture and oxygen movement to plant roots."

MSU Extension, Bert Cregg, "Mulch: Just do it!" (March 21, 2011). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulch_just_do_it [capture 2026-02-14]
- "Use the doughnut technique and keep a six-inch mulch-free ring around the tree base." "For un-composted ground leaves, use only one to two inches to reduce matting."

MSU Smart Gardening tip sheet, Finneran, Krans and Walton, "Smart Gardeners Improve Their Soil and Weed Control with Organic Mulch" (January 24, 2018). https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/smart_gardeners_improve_their_soil_and_weed_control_with_organic_mulch [capture 2026-05-18]
- "In a new landscape bed, use 3-4 inches of mulch initially, then topdress each year with only about 1 inch."
- "Under automatic irrigation, wood mulches tend to degrade quicker due to increased microbial activity. Dry spots in the landscape will retain greater proportions of mulch each year requiring smaller applications."
- Thick yearly layers can smother perennials; "Rots may be encouraged with too much organic material at the plant's crown." Dry mulch "cakes together and can become a layer resistant to rainfall and irrigation".
- "choosing a type of mulch is often based on personal preference and appearance" (list includes "color-enhanced").

MSU Extension, Rebecca Finneran, "Mulch overload!" (April 22, 2010). https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/mulch_overload [capture 2026-05-16]
- Volcanoes: "this dark, moist environment is an invitation for root rot and insect invasion. It also increases surface rooting into the mulch itself".
- "The mulch should be applied as a thin 1-inch layer immediately next to the trunk or stems, which gradually gets thicker as you move outward." "If it's possible to develop several groupings of trees or plants into beds you will be much better off."
- Yearly: "apply 1 to 2 inches max. Where mulch applications have become crusted and hard, break up the clods and pull excess out to the edge of the bed or drip line." "Layers upon layers of dried organic mulch can also create an impervious barrier to water and nutrients."
- Note: MSU gives both a 6 inch bare ring (Cregg) and a thin 1 inch layer at the trunk (Finneran). The post states both.
