## Mowing

Source: MSU Extension bulletin E0013, "Mowing Lawn Turf", G.T. Lyman, June 8, 2015.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/mowing-lawn-turf (snapshot 2026-05-11)
- Height: "Current standards suggest between 2 and 3.75 inches." Earlier recommendations of 1.5 inches were common.
- "Lowering the mowing height reduces the root system."
- "Higher cut lawn grasses are more stress tolerant. This is especially important during the summer heat period."
- Taller, denser grass shades the soil surface, "which reduces germination of weed seeds, particularly crabgrass."
- "Sub-optimum mowing heights will lead to more crabgrass pressure." Continual scalping reduces density and opens room for weeds.
- One-third rule: "not to remove more than one-third of the total leaf surface when mowing your lawn. This may require mowing every four to five days during rapid growth in the spring."
- Mowing guide table: cut at 3.0 inches, mow when the grass reaches 4.50 inches (1.50 inches of growth between mowings).
- Clippings: "Return clippings to the lawn whenever possible." "Clippings do not make significant contributions to thatch." They "contain mostly water and break down rapidly, releasing nutrients back into the system." "Excessive accumulation of clippings from infrequent mowing may smother the turf and should be removed."
- Blades: "Dull, poorly adjusted equipment tears rather than cuts the grass, leaving a ready site for disease invasion and giving the lawn a frayed, brownish look."
- "Varying the mowing direction from time to time can reduce wear patterns."
- Mow when the turf is dry; it also eliminates clumping.
- Leaves: in MSU studies, "up to 6 inches of various types of tree leaves were mowed into lawns. No adverse effects were detected." The residue "will sift into the turf within a few weeks and will be unnoticeable in the spring."

Source: "Lawn tips to save you money", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, April 8, 2016.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/lawn_tips_to_save_you_money (snapshot 2026-05-17)
- At a 3 inch setting and the one-third rule, mow when the turf reaches 4.5 inches.
- "Dull mower blades result in fraying and tearing of the leaf blades that result in a poor aesthetic look and increased water loss from the turf."
- Clippings "are about 80 percent water and don't contribute to the thatch layer unless you're leaving clippings in piles on the lawn."

Source: "How to care for rapidly growing lawns", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, July 9, 2014.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/how_to_care_for_rapidly_growing_lawns (snapshot 2025-11-11)
- "The typical once a week mowing schedule may need to be adjusted" to come close to the one-third rule when growth is fast.
- Mowing wet turf "is not a problem for the turf, but may be a problem for your mower"; turning a mower on wet or saturated soil can compact soil or damage turf, a bigger concern for riding mowers.

Source: "Smart watering for lawns: Don't let the lawn squeeze you dry", Gretchen Voyle, MSU Extension, May 10, 2016.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/smart_watering_for_lawns_dont_let_the_lawn_squeeze_you_dry (snapshot 2025-11-14)
- "Set your mower at the highest setting to mow your grass to 3.5 to 4 inches finished height; the longer the blade, the larger the root system. The larger root mass will support a robust, more drought-tolerant plant."

Source: "Irrigation Practices to Preserve Water Quality", G.T. Lyman, MSU Extension, May 28, 2015.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/irrigation-practices-to-preserve-water-quality (snapshot 2025-11-10)
- Growth cycle of Michigan cool season lawns: a spring surge that peaks "during May or June"; in July and August they reduce leaf and root growth; "During late August through October, leaf growth increases" again (the second growth peak). Root growth continues into fall "while soil temperatures remain above freezing."

## Last mow of the year

Source: "Fall yard and garden cleanup tips", MSU Extension Gardening in Michigan, November 3, 2022 (updated from an article by Mary Wilson).
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/october_gardening_tips (snapshot 2025-11-16)
- "Once your lawn stops growing, lower the mowing height for your final mowing. This will help clean up the lawn, prevents grass from being too tall going into the winter and reduces problems with voles and snow mold." MSU turf specialists recommend "lowering the mowing height no more than one notch on your mower (typically half an inch)."
- No calendar date is given. The page ties the last mow to when the grass stops growing.

Source: "Autumn turf tips", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, September 19, 2008.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/autumn_turf_tips (snapshot 2025-11-21)
- The final lower cut comes "When you get past the leaf mulching period in the fall and the turf has essentially stopped its top-growth"; it helps "clean-up any left over leaf mulch or debris."

## Leaves

Source: "Final fall turf chores", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, September 24, 2010.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/final_fall_turf_chores (snapshot 2025-11-20)
- To mulch leaves: sharp blade, mower raised "as high as it will go", mow when leaves are moist from dew but not really wet, and "don't let the leaves pile up too high before you mow. Too high would probably be greater than 3-4 inches of leaf depth on the turf."
- "Mulching leaves helps the turf by returning nutrients and organic matter."

Source: "Mulch Leaves into Turf for a Smart Lawn", Rebecca Finneran, MSU Extension, May 10, 2016.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/mulch_leaves_into_turf_for_a_smart_lawn (snapshot 2026-11-02)
- "when leaves pile up with wet, heavy snow, it can mean disaster for the turf plant below."
- "Up to 6 inches of leaves can be 'mulched' at a time, depending on the type of mower you have."
- Mulching is usually weekly in October, twice in a week after heavy wind.

Source: "It's not too late to mulch fallen leaves into lawns", MSU Extension, November 17, 2014.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/its_not_too_late_to_mulch_fallen_leaves_into_lawns (snapshot 2026-11-03)
- MSU found "lawns do better with a layer of mulched leaves." "Mulching mowers can handle up to 6 inches of leaves at a time in one pass, but a traditional mower might take several trips."

Source: "Fall yard and garden cleanup tips" (above, 2022).
- "Leaves can be raked around woody plants and perennials and left until spring. Leaves will act as an insulator and help overwinter less-hardy perennials. They also provide a home to beneficial insects and spiders."
- "Leave ornamental grass and perennial stems to provide homes for overwintering bees and other beneficial insects."
- Bumble bees, butterflies and other pollinators may shelter under leaves or on woody debris; consider leaving some habitat undisturbed until spring.
- Irrigation: "Blow out or winterize your irrigation system before a hard freeze."

## Spring cleanup

Source: "When is it safe to clean up my yard this spring?", MSU Extension Gardening in Michigan, May 13, 2024.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/when-is-it-safe-to-clean-up-my-yard-this-spring (snapshot 2026-04-12)
- "In Michigan, some pollinators will emerge on the first warm sunny days of the year while others will remain in their winter shelters until June or July."
- Insects shelter "in or on perennial stems, amongst natural mulching materials, and under the soil surface."
- Leaf litter: "When you remove leaf litter in the springtime, use manual methods and work slowly."
- Stems: one option is "to remove the debris during the dormant season (i.e., late winter) and place the cut stems in a location where the insects will be able to emerge naturally."
- Lawn: "Most lawn-care activities, like mowing and light raking, will not have a big impact on this overwintering stage in the spring."
- No single date or temperature is given for spring cleanup. The 50 degree figure that shows up in search results is about something else; not used.

Source: "Early spring lawn activities", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, March 24, 2011.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/early_spring_lawn_activities (snapshot 2026-05-18)
- Light raking with a leaf rake helps matted turf and snow mold spots: "Don't get too aggressive, you don't want to rip up turf, just clean up debris and remove dead tissue."
- "Rolling saturated soils can result in soil compaction."
- Spring is a good time to sharpen mower blades.

Source: "Spring lawn care tips for the do-it yourselfers", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, March 31, 2020.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/spring_lawn_care_tips_the_red_light_green_light_version (snapshot 2026-01-19)
- "lightly rake the turf with a leaf rake, not a garden rake. Raking will remove some of the dead, blighted leaf blades and facilitate turfgrass recovery."
- Matting is normal "in areas where snow was piled."
- On rolling: "it's better to wait until the soils dry then to do it now and risk making a problem worse."

Source: "Matted lawns and snow mold injury", MSU Extension, March 25, 2010.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/matted_lawns_and_snow_mold_injury (snapshot 2026-04-10)
- "In most cases, the snow mold has probably not killed the turf but has simply killed the leaf tissue." A leaf rake opens the canopy so new growth comes through.

Source: "Tips for reseeding lawns in spring", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, April 20, 2018.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/tips_for_reseeding_lawns_in_the_spring (snapshot 2026-02-11)
- Spots of lawn can be "killed from de-icing salt, dog spot or snow mold over the winter."

## Seeding and new lawns

Source: "Establishing a New Lawn Using Seed", Extension bulletin E2910, Suleiman Bughrara, MSU Extension, February 18, 2016.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/establishing_a_new_lawn_using_seed_e2910 (snapshot 2026-02-12)
- Timing: "Late summer and early fall plantings produce" the strongest results in Michigan (MSU's word is "best"). The window MSU names: "from August 15 to September 15. The warm days and cool nights are ideal for seed germination and seedling growth."
- Spring is second, "no later than June 15." "If you cannot water your lawn, do not seed your lawn in late spring or early summer." Weed invasion "can be a problem, particularly in the spring." In late spring or early summer plantings, "crabgrass can be a problem."
- "Seed grown plants have stronger root systems initially."
- New builds: "When building a new home, keep your topsoil in a pile off to the side. After the building is complete, spread it evenly over the lawn area."
- Grade: "The final grade or slope of the lawn should be away from the building, and good surface drainage should be evident. Normally, a fall of 1 foot every 50 feet will keep excess water from standing on the surface of the lawn. Too great a slope may lead to erosion, difficulties in mowing and possible scalping."
- Root zone: topsoil of "at least 4 inches." Remove "stones, roots, large soil clods and other material."
- Work the soil only when it is dry enough not to stick to implements or compact.
- Rake seed in lightly so about 10 percent still shows; light rolling firms soil around the seed.
- Straw: clean wheat, oat or barley straw, not rye. "only one layer of straw ... This will usually require about two, 60-pound bales per 1,000 sq. ft. You should be able to see the soil surface between the straw stems. Mulch put on at this rate may be left on the ground to decompose. Heavier mulches that completely cover the soil should be removed as soon as you see grass blades emerging." "If seeding is done in late fall, the mulch should be left on during the winter."
- Watering: frequent light watering; do not let the seedbed dry out or get soggy; "you may need to apply water two or three times per day" on warm, sunny days. About a week after sowing, water less often but more per watering. "Normally, a 2-month-old turf may be watered the same as an established lawn."
- First mow: "Mow as soon as grass blades exceed 3 inches in height." Keep blades sharp. "Keep traffic off the new lawn until the new turf has filled in enough that you cannot see the soil. Until then, the only traffic on the turf should be you and your mower."

Source: "Tips for seeding lawns in September", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, September 12, 2019.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/tips_for_seeding_lawns_in_september (snapshot 2026-04-13)
- "September is the ideal time for seeding turfgrass in Michigan." Reasons: "Reduced weed competition from summer annuals such as crabgrass, cooler temperatures and shorter day length that results in less time for soil drying."
- Seed broadcast without contact with soil "will feed birds, but not be effective for growing new turfgrass."
- "Mowing turf helps it spread laterally and fill the area."

Source: "Late season lawn seeding", MSU Extension Gardening in Michigan, September 24, 2012 (updated from Gary Heilig).
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/late_season_lawn_seeding (snapshot 2025-11-10)
- "In Michigan, mid-August through September is the ideal time to repair or seed a new lawn, but if the weather is favorable, it may be possible to seed into early October." October is riskier: seed may sprout without time to establish.

Source: "Autumn turf tips" (2008, above) and "Final fall turf chores" (2010, above).
- "Along the Grand Rapids-Lansing-Detroit corridor you are probably safe to seed until around October 1 ... If you're north of that line and still want to seed, hope for a warm fall." Grand Blanc (about 42.93 N) is north of Lansing (about 42.73 N) and Detroit (about 42.33 N), so it is north of that line.

Source: "Time to seed", Kevin Frank, MSU Extension, August 25, 2006.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/time_to_seed (snapshot 2025-11-22)
- Older figure for overseeding: 1 bale of straw per 1,000 square feet, with 1/3 to 1/2 of the soil still visible. Not used; the 2016 bulletin's figure is used instead.

## Sprinklers and watering

Source: "Smart watering for lawns" (Voyle, 2016, above).
- "A lawn usually requires 1 inch of water per week. It doesn't make a difference whether it falls from the sky or comes out of a hose. Buy a rain gauge."
- "Watering should be divided into several applications per week. On sandy soils, it may be every other day. On heavier soils, it may be every third day."
- "It is best to water during daylight hours. Watering in the evening creates wet grass and with warm nights can enable certain lawn fungal diseases to become active."
- "More advanced systems have a built-in rain gauge and will adjust automatically to rain amounts."

Source: "Irrigation Practices to Preserve Water Quality" (Lyman, 2015, above).
- "Generally, cool-season lawn turf requires 0.5 to 1.5 inches of water per week." More in heat, full sun and wind. Rain counts.
- "Light, frequent applications of water are much more productive than heavy (soaking) applications once a week."
- Measure a system: scatter rain gauges or tuna cans, "Turn on the system for one hour and measure the amount water collected in each container."
- "the delivery rate of the irrigation system should never exceed the infiltration rate of the soil." Compacted clay can run off on slopes; "On sloping lawns, using shorter cycles with repetition will permit time for infiltration and reduce the potential for runoff."
- Under restrictions, "irrigating from midnight until 6 a.m. is acceptable." "irrigating during the early evening hours is one of the worst times to irrigate."
- "Install a rain over-ride device on your irrigation system to prevent wasting water. During rainy periods turn off a clock-controlled irrigation system."
- Dormancy: extended warm, dry weather makes turf go dormant ("stops growth and turns brown"); the crown "remains alive." Irrigation can keep turf green through summer. "an abrupt change in watering practices from regular irrigation to no irrigation during the heat stress period might be harmful to the turf. Dormancy must be induced gradually."

Source: "Lawn irrigation tips", MSU Extension, July 8, 2020.
https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/lawn-irrigation-tips (snapshot 2026-01-15)
- MSU advocates more frequent, lighter watering than the old once a week soak; under restrictions, every second or third day at about 0.25 inch, early morning.
